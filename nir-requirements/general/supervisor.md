# General NIR and Project Requirements — Supervisor Guide

## What a strong project must provide

The project must be formulated so that the student can produce a complete, verifiable, and independent result within the allotted period. Merely implementing a model or prototype is not enough: the project must allow the committee to verify the relevance of the problem, the quality of the result, and its difference from existing solutions.

The universal structure is:

**problem → demonstrated relevance → alternatives → limitation or gap → original contribution → method or architecture → experimental or practical validation → metrics → quantitative comparison → analysis → conclusion.**

For multi-component systems, plan an analysis of key component contributions from the outset.

## What to define in the project statement

- a specific problem rather than a broad field;
- inputs, outputs, constraints, and success criteria;
- the project type and its specific criteria;
- a minimum substantive and verifiable result;
- a set of relevant alternatives or baselines;
- metrics and conditions for a fair comparison;
- an experimental or practical design;
- mandatory artifacts;
- reproducibility criteria; and
- the expected level of student independence.

## How formal caps can undermine a project

Supervisors should look for critical caps rather than the work's average quality:

- a missing mandatory artifact prevents 5A;
- no quantitative comparison limits the grade to 4C;
- no substantive baseline limits the grade to 3D;
- no ablation or component analysis for a complex system limits the grade to 4C;
- an unverifiable repository limits the grade to 4C;
- unproven relevance limits the grade to 4C;
- inability to explain a substantial part of the work limits the grade to 3D; and
- no working or verifiable result limits the grade to 3E or 2FX.

The final grade is the highest level for which all critical requirements are met, not the arithmetic mean of individual criteria.

## Supervisor checkpoints

### At the start of the project

Verify:

- that the problem is genuinely relevant;
- that the project is not reduced to “run a model” or “assemble a service”;
- that clear alternatives and a viable comparison exist;
- that metrics are available;
- that the scope permits a complete result; and
- that the project type reflects the nature of the result rather than convenient presentation.

### Before the midpoint

The following should be visible:

- a literature review and list of strong alternatives;
- a working baseline;
- a validated data or experiment pipeline;
- a repository with a meaningful development history;
- initial measurable results; and
- refined success criteria.

### Before the defense

Verify:

- reproducibility of the main results;
- quantitative comparison under comparable conditions;
- consistency between conclusions and data;
- limitations and negative results;
- the quality of the README, launch process, dependencies, and tests;
- the presence of all specific artifacts; and
- the student's ability to explain the method, architecture, metrics, baseline, and causes of the results.

For a scientific project, the supervisor's feedback and grade constitute admission to the NIR defense. The supervisor's absence or missing feedback before the defense results in 2FX.

## Repository

When a project includes software, the supervisor must require a repository that supports verification of the result rather than merely displaying code: a README, installation and launch instructions, dependencies, configurations, tests where necessary, reproducible experiments, organized results, and a commit history spanning the semester.

## Defense

The presentation must make an evidence-based case rather than narrate a chronology of work. The recommended structure is: problem → alternatives → gap → contribution → method → experiment → comparison → result → limitations → conclusion.

A strong defense requires the student to justify decisions instead of concealing a lack of understanding behind working code or automatically generated text.

## Organizational context

A project is treated as complete work that may be continued in a subsequent project. The project type determines the expected artifacts and main focus of evaluation. Additional achievements—including publications, patents, deployments, technology publications, and open-source development—may strengthen the final grade but do not replace mandatory requirements.

Generative AI is permitted except for falsification of results; responsibility for errors remains with the student.
